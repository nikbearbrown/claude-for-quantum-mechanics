## Preface

For most of the twentieth century, the strangest parts of quantum mechanics — entanglement, the measurement problem, the question of what the wave function *is* — were treated as philosophy, safely deferred to the final week of the course and then skipped for time. That era is over. Entanglement is now a budgeted engineering resource. Decoherence times are printed in the abstracts of corporate research papers. Bell inequality violations have moved from thought experiment to Nobel citation to undergraduate laboratory. The 2020s turned the foundations of quantum mechanics into a technology sector, and the textbooks have not caught up.

This volume exists because quantum mechanics education needs rebuilding for the quantum information era, and patching a 1960s curriculum with a bonus chapter on qubits is not rebuilding. The traditional course teaches pure states evolving unitarily in splendid isolation — and then a student opens a real paper and meets density matrices, Lindblad dissipators, $T_1$ and $T_2$ times, logical error rates, and CHSH parameters in the first paragraph. The gap between the course and the literature has become a gap between the course and the field. We wrote this book to close it: density matrices arrive in Chapter 1, not as an afterthought; open systems and decoherence get a full treatment rather than a hand wave; measurement and interpretation are handled as physics constrained by experiment; and the capstone asks the student to reconstruct the central result of a current research paper, because that — not another idealized exercise — is what literacy now means.

Why now, specifically? Because the field crossed a threshold while this series was being written. Logical qubits below the error-correction threshold moved from theorem to hardware. Students entering physics, engineering, and computer science in 2026 will spend their careers in a world where quantum devices are instruments, products, and security threats — and where AI tools sit beside them as they learn. A textbook for these students must teach not only the formalism but the judgment: what a hardware paper actually claims, what an interpretation actually explains, what a machine-generated derivation actually got wrong. Every chapter here ends with simulations the student builds with an LLM as coding partner, and the introduction is frank about where that partnership helps and where it hollows out learning.

Why these authors? One of us is an experimental physicist whose career began in exactly the physics this volume formalizes — quantum chaos in microwave billiards, superconductivity, and the collective behavior of quantum systems coupled to messy environments — long before "decoherence" was a budget line. The other builds AI systems and has spent years teaching students to work with, and to distrust, intelligent machines. Between the two sits this book: the physics of information, measured honestly, taught for the era that will use it.

— Nik Bear Brown and Srinivas Sridhar, Boston, 2026
