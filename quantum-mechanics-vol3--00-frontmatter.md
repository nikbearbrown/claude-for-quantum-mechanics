## Preface

The first two volumes of this series taught quantum mechanics the way the subject is usually introduced: exactly solvable problems, clean Hamiltonians, closed-form answers. The square well, the harmonic oscillator, the hydrogen atom. Those problems are the foundation, and they are also a kind of beautiful lie — because almost nothing in nature is exactly solvable. Helium is not. Ammonia is not. A transistor is not. The working physicist spends most of a career on systems whose Schrödinger equations have no closed-form solutions, and the craft of that career is approximation: knowing which method applies, what its small parameter is, and — most importantly — how large the error is in what you threw away.

That craft is what this volume exists to teach. Perturbation theory, the variational principle, WKB, time-dependent transitions, scattering, and the band structure of solids are not a grab bag of techniques. They are a single discipline practiced in different regimes, and we have tried to present them that way: every chapter asks the same three questions. What is the solvable skeleton? What is the small parameter? Where does the approximation break? The capstone chapter asks you to put the whole toolkit to work on a real system — to produce a number with units, compare it to a measurement, and account for the difference.

Why write this now? Because the way students learn physics has changed faster than the books they learn from. A self-learner in the AI era has, in a chat window, a tireless assistant that can integrate, differentiate, and produce plausible-sounding derivations on demand. That assistant makes a strong student stronger and a passive student weaker. A textbook for this moment has to do more than present material; it has to structure the encounter between the student, the physics, and the machine. That is why every chapter in this series ends with simulation exercises designed to be built with an LLM as a coding partner — and why the introduction that follows says, bluntly, when not to ask the machine for help. Approximation methods are an ideal proving ground for this discipline, because the entire subject is about error control, and error control is exactly what a student must learn to apply to AI output.

Why these authors? One of us has spent decades doing the physics this book teaches — measuring quantum chaos in microwave cavities, superconductivity, and collective excitations in real materials, where approximation methods are not homework but the daily instrument of discovery. The other has spent a career building AI systems and teaching students how to learn with them, and how not to be fooled by them. This volume sits exactly at the intersection: real systems, controlled approximations, and a new kind of learner. We wrote it for that learner.

— Nik Bear Brown and Srinivas Sridhar, Boston, 2026
