## Preface

There is a moment in every physicist's education when quantum mechanics changes character. In a first course, the subject is wave mechanics: you solve differential equations for ψ(x), you sketch wells and barriers, and the mathematics, however strange its conclusions, looks like the mathematics you already know. Then comes the second course, and suddenly the objects are kets, the verbs are operators, and entire spectra are derived from commutation relations without anyone solving a differential equation at all. Many students experience this shift as a loss — the comfortable pictures vanish into abstraction. This book was written to make the shift feel like what it actually is: a promotion. The formalism is not a more pretentious notation for things you already knew. It is the machinery that makes three-dimensional atoms, spin, and the periodic table solvable at all.

This is the second volume of a five-volume quantum mechanics series built for self-learners and for students studying in the AI era. The series exists because the tools available to a physics student changed abruptly: capable language models can now produce working simulations, check algebra, and explain steps on demand. That changes what a textbook should do. It should hand the student derivations worth following actively, simulations worth building personally, and the verification habits — checking Hermiticity, confirming normalization, testing limiting cases — that keep AI-generated work honest. Every chapter here ends with simulation exercises that carry forward the system established in Volume 1, so that by the final chapter the reader has assembled, validated, and can defend a working model of a multi-electron atom.

Why now is also a question about the subject itself. Spin, two-level systems, and entangled angular momenta were once the austere back half of a quantum course; they are now the working vocabulary of quantum computing and quantum sensing. A student who genuinely owns the Bloch sphere, the Pauli algebra, and the addition of angular momenta is holding the entry ticket to an entire industry that did not exist when the standard textbooks were written.

As for why these authors: this book is a collaboration between a computer scientist who builds AI infrastructure for education and a physicist whose four decades of research — from quantum chaos and superconductivity to nanophotonics — have lived inside the very formalism this volume teaches. One of us has spent years learning how students actually use AI tools, and how those tools fail them; the other has spent a career using operator methods on real laboratory systems and teaching them to generations of students. The book is our joint answer to a question we kept asking each other: what should a second course in quantum mechanics look like, now?

We assume the reader has completed a first course covering one-dimensional wave mechanics, at the level of Volume 1 of this series. Mathematical gaps are covered just-in-time by Volume 5, *Math for Quantum Physics*, indexed to each chapter. The book does not cover perturbation theory, scattering, or quantum information in depth; those belong to later volumes. What it covers, it covers completely: from the first bra to the built atom.
